How does the report rank happiness?

The World Happiness Report ranks countries and territories by life evaluation. Gallup asks respondents to rate their current life as a whole on a scale from 0 to 10. Hong Kong averaged 5.57 in the 2023–2025 surveys, placing 90th out of 147 countries and territories.

The report uses six factors to examine why scores vary across places, including GDP per capita, healthy life expectancy, social support, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption. Survey responses determine the rankings. The six factors help explain differences in scores. Read Chapter 2 ↗

The global pattern

Places with a larger model contribution from GDP per capita tend to report higher life evaluations. Their scores vary widely around the dashed line. All 17 Latin American countries highlighted here score higher than predicted, by 0.75 points on average. The five places in East and Southeast Asia with the largest GDP contributions score at or below the prediction, by 0.57 points on average. Taiwan sits close to the line. Hong Kong is 1.18 points below it, the largest shortfall of the five.

Hong Kong ranks high on GDP but scores close to Indonesia

We compare the five places in East and Southeast Asia with the largest GDP-per-capita contributions in the World Happiness Report. Within the region, Hong Kong ranks second on this measure, behind Singapore. The global trend predicts a score of about 6.75 for Hong Kong. Residents gave their lives an average of 5.57, a shortfall of 1.18 points and the largest among the five.

Hong Kong’s GDP-per-capita contribution is 0.448 points higher than Indonesia’s (1.960 versus 1.512). Adjusted for purchasing power, Hong Kong’s GDP per capita in 2024 was about 4.6 times Indonesia’s. World Bank data ↗

GDP per capita contributes about 1.96 points in both Hong Kong and Taiwan. Taiwan’s life evaluation is about 1.15 points higher.

Hong Kong leads Taiwan on the six-factor total

GDP per capita contributes almost equally in Hong Kong and Taiwan. Hong Kong has larger model contributions from healthy life expectancy and perceptions of corruption. Taiwan has larger contributions from social support, freedom to make life choices and generosity. Hong Kong leads by 0.362 points across all six factors. The survey results reverse that order, with scores of 5.57 for Hong Kong and 6.71 for Taiwan.

Hong Kong falls below the prediction.
What might explain the gap?

Hong Kong’s six factors add up to more than Taiwan’s, but its residents rate their lives lower. The six-factor model predicts about 7.03 for Hong Kong. Residents gave their lives an average of 5.57, about 1.46 points below the prediction.

Local data offer two clues. The census shows large differences in living space across households. A household survey finds that loneliness is more common among people aged 80 and over than among younger seniors. How do these conditions relate to the way people rate their lives?

In subdivided units, the median is just 6 m² per person

The median living space per person in subdivided units is about 40% of the citywide median. A study of 1,480 Hong Kong adults found that housing costs, crowding and living conditions were associated with subjective well-being. The link was especially strong among private renters. Hong Kong housing study ↗

About one in five people aged 80 and over felt lonely

In the Hong Kong–Taiwan comparison, social support contributed 0.116 points less to Hong Kong’s model score. The World Happiness Report measures social support by asking whether people have family or friends they can count on if they are in trouble.

Hong Kong’s household survey offers a closer look at loneliness. The share who felt lonely in the week before the survey was 8.9 percentage points higher among people aged 80 and over than among those aged 65–79. A study that followed 200 older adults in Hong Kong also linked social networks and participation to life satisfaction. Longitudinal study of older adults ↗

How does economic strength translate into better lives?

Hong Kong ranks high on GDP per capita and health, yet residents rate their lives lower than the model predicts. Local research points to the homes people can afford and the support they can count on. The evidence cannot quantify how much of Hong Kong’s score gap each factor explains.
